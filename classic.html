Robotic Surface Bioimpedance Scanning with Ergodic Control

Dynamical Systems Lab · Duke University · May 2026 – Present

I'm teaching a robot arm (a UR3e) to scan things by touch. A camera on its wrist maps the surface, a planner figures out where the probe should spend its time, and the arm presses down with a steady 2 N while it follows that path. Eventually I want to measure bioimpedance (basically how much tissue resists a tiny electrical current) along these paths to help catch skin cancer.

Still a work in progress. Planning and tracing work end to end on the robot. The bioimpedance sensor works too, but I haven't hooked it into a scan yet.

Python UR3e robot arm Path planning JAX Force control Camera calibration Point clouds (Open3D) OpenCV Statistics Bluetooth LE Hardware testing

How a Scan Runs

The cleaning step spits out two things: an ink-only version for the planner, and a 3D version the robot uses to get the probe's height and angle right at every point.

Planning the Path

How the path is planned

A simple back-and-forth scan treats every spot the same, so most of its time would go to blank paper. Ergodic planning fixes that: the plan spends time in each area in proportion to how much "information" is there. Here, the information is the dark ink.

E=−2T∫0Tp(x(t))dt+1T2∫0T∫0Tk(x(t),x(τ))dtdτ
  1. Score the scan. Every point gets a score for how dark it is, so ink scores high and white paper gets zero. Each score is averaged over a 1 cm-radius circle, the patch the planner assumes the probe can sense.
  2. Turn the goal into a formula. E (above) goes down when the path sits on high scores and its points don't bunch up. There's also a small penalty so it doesn't make big, fast moves.
  3. Make a first guess. Pick 121 random points (more likely where the ink is) and connect them into a short route with 2-opt, a classic traveling-salesman trick.
  4. Improve it. An optimizer called iLQR keeps nudging the path until E stops going down. It runs in JAX on a GPU and takes about a minute.
  5. Back to 3D. Each point gets its height from the scan, so the robot gets 121 targets it can actually touch.

The planner is based on a paper (Sun et al., IEEE T-RO 2025), and the core code came from my lab. My job was getting it to actually work on our robot. I rewrote some helper functions we were missing, sped it up enough to handle a full scan (faster point lookups, 2 mm downsampling, and running it on a GPU took it from about 9 minutes to 1), and fixed what it counts as information (more on that below).

Path planning · Optimization · JAX

Fixing what the planner pays attention to

At first the paths kept wandering onto blank paper and tape. Turns out the camera sees white paper as light gray, so the planner thought blank paper held 31% of all the information and spent about 30% of every path there. The blue tape looked like ink too. Once I subtracted each scan's paper level and masked out the tape, the path moved onto the shapes, and I didn't have to touch the planner's math at all. Top left is before, top right is after.

Debugging · Point clouds

Cleaning up the scan first (trajectories 15–17)

I used the same scan and settings each time and only changed which parts got painted white before planning. Painting colored stuff white (16) got rid of the lid edge and tape, but it left gray specks off the sheet and the path ended on one of them. Painting everything outside the sheet white (17) kept the whole path on the paper and made it about a quarter shorter.

Path execution · Visualization

How the arm holds 2 N

Every 50 ms the robot gets one motion command that mixes three things: keep moving along the path, push in or back off to hold 2 N, and tilt to match the surface. The camera points sideways and can't actually see where the probe touches, so the force sensor is the only thing telling the arm how hard it's pressing.

Control systems · ur_rtde

Fixing a shaky force loop

With the gain I started with, the force shook about twice a second on the stiffer parts of the box lid, and one run shut itself off after going over 6 N. Whether it shakes depends on the gain times how stiff the surface is, so now the arm presses in a bit when it first touches down, measures the stiffness, and picks the gain from that. If it still starts shaking, it cuts the gain in half. I tested both fixes in a simulation built from these logs.

Control tuning · Simulation

How fast can it go?

I measured the force error on 278 path segments across 9 runs and fit a mixed-effects model to it (basically a regression that also accounts for sharp turns, surface stiffness, and differences between runs). Faster means more error, no surprise there. For a typical segment, the model says I can go up to about 9.5 mm/s and stay under my 0.5 N limit, but about half the real segments near 10 mm/s still went over. So far the best setting gives each step 1.5 s and caps the speed at 15 mm/s.

Statistics · Experiment design

A real planned scan on the robot

This is trajectory 21 running on the robot: 905 mm across five circles in 108 s at a steady 10 mm/s, holding 1.97 ± 0.66 N. Since the speed never changed, the four long jumps between circles ate up 34% of the time, even though they were only 3% of the plan. Most of the force error came from the tight zigzags inside each circle, which turn sharply about every 5 mm.

Safety checks

Safety · Robustness

Camera Calibration

Figuring out where the camera is

Every scan depends on knowing exactly where the camera sits on the wrist. I figured it out two different ways: with a printed target (a ChArUco board) seen from 20 angles, and by touching points with the probe, since the arm's joint sensors know where the tip is without needing the camera at all. I'm using the touch method now, and it's typically off by about 6 mm (6.2 mm median).

OpenCV · Calibration · Testing on new data

A calibration bug that looked fine

OpenCV changed how these boards are laid out in version 4.7. Our robot runs 4.5 and the board I printed came from a newer version, so 5 of its 20 corners got matched to the wrong squares, even though the detection looked perfect. I caught it by checking how well the corners fit a flat board: 223 px off, vs. 0.29 px for a correctly made one.

Debugging · Computer vision

Bioimpedance Sensor

Getting the bioimpedance sensor working

I got the lab's MAX30009 sensor board streaming over Bluetooth to the robot's computer. That meant fixing a few bugs in its driver (a typo that broke connecting and two arguments in the wrong order) and making it retry the Bluetooth scan, since the board barely advertises itself and one 10 s scan wasn't enough to find it. The calibration checks came out to 899.95 Ω (should be 900) and 999.97 Ω (should be 1000).

Nasal Cannula Detection Device

Healthcare & Embedded Systems · Aug 2025 – Feb 2026

A ~$30 Arduino device that detects when an infant's nasal cannula slips out and alerts nurses, built for wards like Mulago National Referral Hospital in Uganda, where one nurse may cover up to 35 babies.

Arduino / C++ Embedded firmware Sensor calibration Circuit design CAD & 3D printing Device testing
Firmware on GitHub →

How It Works

  • Sensing: A thermistor at the nose samples every 500 ms against a baseline set during a 5-second calibration.
  • Detection: The alarm fires only after 10 consecutive readings below 99.5% of baseline, which filters out false alarms.
  • Hardware: A 3D-printed housing holds the circuit, battery, 16×2 LCD, reset buttons, and status LEDs for power, calibration, and dislodgement.

Results

  • 100% true positives across 60 trials
  • 17.29 s average time to alert
  • 21.86 s average attachment time
  • Disassembled 50+ times with no structural wear

Exoplanet Detection & Habitable Zone Analysis

Astrophysics & Data Science Research

Used TESS transit photometry from the MAST archive to detect exoplanets and test whether they fall within their star's habitable zone.

Python Lightkurve Astropy Time-series analysis Signal detection

Methodology

  • Transit photometry: Detected brightness dips as planets cross their star.
  • Light curves: Processed and normalized TESS pixel files with Lightkurve.
  • Periodograms: Found orbital periods with Box Least Squares (BLS).
  • Habitable zones: Computed from stellar mass–luminosity relations with Astropy.
  • False positives: Screened with centroid tracking, background flux, and eclipsing-binary checks.

Key Findings

  • Of 2,537 documented exoplanets, only 118 (4.7%) lie within habitable zones.
  • Most discovered exoplanets orbit very close to their host stars.
  • Flagged TIC 34798133 as a promising candidate for follow-up.

FOAK Fungal-Detection Device (MycoSpec)

Medical Hardware & Embedded Diagnostics · Nov 2025 – Present

A clip-on HVAC sensor that tracks VOCs, temperature, and humidity to flag Aspergillus mold risk for immunocompromised patients before growth is visible.

Embedded sensing VOC sensors Data logging Risk classification Live dashboard Hardware design
Full engineering documentation →

Why It Matters

HVAC systems are warm, humid, and dark, which is ideal for mold. For transplant and chemotherapy patients, inhaled Aspergillus spores can cause invasive aspergillosis, which kills more than 50% of those infected. MycoSpec clips onto standard vent fins with no tools or downtime.

Testing & Results

  • Logged ~350 s of sensor data alongside live Aspergillus fumigatus cultures in the lab.
  • Found distinct VOC bands separating the negative (no mold) and positive controls.
  • Turned those bands into LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH risk thresholds on a live dashboard.

Key Challenges

  • Specificity: Separating mold VOCs from cleaning agents, occupant activity, and off-gassing materials.
  • Species: Telling species such as Aspergillus and Stachybotrys apart by their VOC signatures.
